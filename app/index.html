      What a surviving spouse can claim, and how to apply

      Know what's already there

      Social Security Survivor Benefits

      If your spouse dies, Social Security may pay you a monthly survivor benefit — but almost nothing about it is automatic. Here's the plain version.

      Who can claim, and when. A widow or widower can claim survivor benefits on their spouse's record as early as age 60 — or age 50 if disabled. Claiming early pays a reduced amount; waiting pays more.

      The lump sum is $255. There's a one-time death payment of $255, and it must be applied for — generally within 2 years of the death. It does not arrive on its own.

      Benefits are not automatic. You have to apply. You can start by phone with Social Security at 1-800-772-1213.

      Remarriage matters. Remarrying before age 60 usually ends eligibility for survivor benefits on a late spouse's record. Remarrying after 60 does not.

      Kids may qualify too. Children under 18 may also be eligible for benefits on a parent's record.

      Timing can pay. In some cases it pays more to take the survivor benefit now and switch to your own retirement benefit later — or the reverse. That choice is worth a real conversation before you file.

      What the VA pays for — and what it doesn’t

      For veterans & their families

      What the VA Actually Covers

      Many veterans believe “the VA takes care of all of it.” The VA covers some important things — honorably and at no cost. It does not cover the rest. Here's the honest split.

      What the VA covers. An eligible veteran can be buried in a national cemetery at no cost to the family: the gravesite, opening and closing, a headstone or marker, a burial flag, and a Presidential Memorial Certificate. Our local veterans cemetery: the Southern Nevada Veterans Memorial Cemetery in Boulder City.

      What the VA does not cover. The VA does not pay the funeral home bill, the service, the casket, or the family's other costs. A partial burial allowance exists only in specific cases — most families can't count on it.

      The key paper is the DD-214. Eligibility runs through the DD-214. Keep a copy where your family can find it — the Where Everything Lives index below is built for exactly that.

      Confirm it ahead of time. Veterans can confirm eligibility before it's needed with the VA's Pre-Need application (Form 40-10007) at va.gov — so your family never has to wonder in a hard week.

      The VA honors the burial. The funeral home bill still lands on your family — and that's the part a policy covers.

      Why today: Pre-need eligibility, a findable DD-214, and coverage for the costs the VA doesn't pay — all three are calm-week jobs. None of them can be done in the week they're needed.

      The four legal papers every family should have in place

      Beyond coverage

      Four Documents Every Family Needs

      Coverage is one leg of the stool. These four documents are the others — plain basics every family should know.

      1. A will — and a guardian for your kids. A will says where things go. For parents, it does something bigger: it names who raises your minor children. Ask it directly: if both of you were gone, who raises your kids? If the answer takes more than five seconds, that's the first thing to fix.

      2. Beneficiary designations — kept current. For life insurance, the beneficiary form overrides the will. The policy pays the name on file, which is why the checkup on this page asks about life changes — marriage, divorce, remarriage all matter here.

      3. An advance directive. Your healthcare wishes, in writing, so your family isn't guessing about medical decisions in a hospital hallway.

      4. A financial power of attorney. Naming who can handle money matters if you're alive but unable to — bills, accounts, the house — without a court fight.

      Review them when life changes: after a marriage, a divorce, a birth, a death, or a move. A plan from three life-chapters ago is a plan for a family that no longer exists.
