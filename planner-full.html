Your state

Nevada

For veterans: the Southern Nevada Veterans Memorial Cemetery in Boulder City is Nevada's state veterans cemetery — burial there is at no cost for eligible veterans. See the Veterans section below for what the VA does and doesn't cover.

Property basics: Nevada is a community-property state. Plainly put: most of what a married couple acquires during the marriage belongs to both spouses equally. When one spouse dies, the survivor automatically keeps their half — the other half passes by will or, without one, by state law.

Good to know in Nevada

  • You can put in writing who may make your funeral arrangements — a signed designation that Nevada funeral homes must honor ahead of the usual next-of-kin order.
  • Death certificates are ordered through the funeral home or the Nevada Office of Vital Records. Order several certified copies — banks and insurers each need one.
  • Nevada has no state estate tax and no inheritance tax.
  • Nevada offers a faster, simpler probate process for smaller estates — one more reason a written plan here stays simple.

For veterans: Texas State Veterans Cemeteries in Abilene, Corpus Christi, Killeen, and Mission — plus national cemeteries in Dallas–Fort Worth, Houston, and San Antonio (Fort Sam Houston) — bury eligible veterans at no cost. See the Veterans section below for what the VA does and doesn't cover.

Property basics: Texas is a community-property state. Plainly put: most of what a married couple acquires during the marriage belongs to both spouses equally. When one spouse dies, the survivor automatically keeps their half — the other half passes by will or, without one, by state law.

Good to know in Texas

  • Texas law lets you name, in writing, the person who may control your funeral arrangements — that designation comes ahead of the standard next-of-kin order.
  • Death certificates are ordered through the funeral home or Texas Vital Statistics. Order several certified copies — banks and insurers each need one.
  • Texas has no state estate tax and no inheritance tax.
  • Texas probate is known for being comparatively streamlined when a will names an independent executor — a will written that way saves your family time.

For veterans: Florida National Cemetery in Bushnell and Sarasota National Cemetery bury eligible veterans at no cost. See the Veterans section below for what the VA does and doesn't cover.

Property basics: Florida is not a community-property state. Instead, Florida's homestead protections for a surviving spouse are among the strongest in the country: your primary home gets special protection, and it generally can't be willed away from a surviving spouse or minor children.

Good to know in Florida

  • Florida law sets a priority order for who may make funeral arrangements — and a person you name in writing comes first.
  • Death certificates are ordered through the funeral home or the Florida Bureau of Vital Statistics. Order several certified copies — banks and insurers each need one.
  • Florida has no state estate tax and no inheritance tax.
  • Because the homestead rules are so specific, Florida families who own a home should review their will and their beneficiaries together — the two have to agree.

    Know what's already there

    Social Security Survivor Benefits

    If your spouse dies, Social Security may pay you a monthly survivor benefit — but almost nothing about it is automatic. Here's the plain version.

    Who can claim, and when. A widow or widower can claim survivor benefits on their spouse's record as early as age 60 — or age 50 if disabled. Claiming early pays a reduced amount; waiting pays more.

    The lump sum is $255. There's a one-time death payment of $255, and it must be applied for — generally within 2 years of the death. It does not arrive on its own.

    Benefits are not automatic. You have to apply. You can start by phone with Social Security at 1-800-772-1213.

    Remarriage matters. Remarrying before age 60 usually ends eligibility for survivor benefits on a late spouse's record. Remarrying after 60 does not.

    Kids may qualify too. Children under 18 may also be eligible for benefits on a parent's record.

    Timing can pay. In some cases it pays more to take the survivor benefit now and switch to your own retirement benefit later — or the reverse. That choice is worth a real conversation before you file.

    What applying takes

    Survivor benefits help — but they're a fraction of a paycheck. They were never designed to replace an income or pay a funeral bill on their own. That gap is exactly what coverage is for.

    Social Security gives your spouse a partial cushion and a $255 payment — after paperwork, after a phone call, after a wait. A policy pays your beneficiary directly, in days, with no application queue.

    Why today: The survivor benefit is whatever it is — you can't change it now. Your own coverage is the one part of this picture still in your hands, priced by today's age and health.

    For veterans & their families

    What the VA Actually Covers

    Many veterans believe “the VA takes care of all of it.” The VA covers some important things — honorably and at no cost. It does not cover the rest. Here's the honest split.

    What the VA covers. An eligible veteran can be buried in a national cemetery at no cost to the family: the gravesite, opening and closing, a headstone or marker, a burial flag, and a Presidential Memorial Certificate. Our local veterans cemetery: the Southern Nevada Veterans Memorial Cemetery in Boulder City.

    What the VA does not cover. The VA does not pay the funeral home bill, the service, the casket, or the family's other costs. A partial burial allowance exists only in specific cases — most families can't count on it.

    The key paper is the DD-214. Eligibility runs through the DD-214. Keep a copy where your family can find it — the Where Everything Lives index below is built for exactly that.

    Confirm it ahead of time. Veterans can confirm eligibility before it's needed with the VA's Pre-Need application (Form 40-10007) at va.gov — so your family never has to wonder in a hard week.

    The VA honors the burial. The funeral home bill still lands on your family — and that's the part a policy covers.

    Why today: Pre-need eligibility, a findable DD-214, and coverage for the costs the VA doesn't pay — all three are calm-week jobs. None of them can be done in the week they're needed.

    Beyond coverage

    Four Documents Every Family Needs

    Coverage is one leg of the stool. These four documents are the others — plain basics every family should know.

    1. A will — and a guardian for your kids. A will says where things go. For parents, it does something bigger: it names who raises your minor children. Ask it directly: if both of you were gone, who raises your kids? If the answer takes more than five seconds, that's the first thing to fix.

    2. Beneficiary designations — kept current. For life insurance, the beneficiary form overrides the will. The policy pays the name on file, which is why the checkup on this page asks about life changes — marriage, divorce, remarriage all matter here.

    3. An advance directive. Your healthcare wishes, in writing, so your family isn't guessing about medical decisions in a hospital hallway.

    4. A financial power of attorney. Naming who can handle money matters if you're alive but unable to — bills, accounts, the house — without a court fight.

    Review them when life changes: after a marriage, a divorce, a birth, a death, or a move. A plan from three life-chapters ago is a plan for a family that no longer exists.

    This is education, not legal advice — an attorney finalizes legal documents. Christopher handles the coverage side: the policy, the beneficiaries, and the amounts.
