Why I do this work

I've lived the other side of this conversation

In 2018, I lost my wife Jessica suddenly — a brain aneurysm, at 48 years old. We had been married 15 years. We had no life insurance in place.

On top of the grief, I was suddenly raising our son alone, on one income, figuring out funeral costs and everything that came after. Nobody should have to do math like that in the worst week of their life.

That's why I do this work, and why I do it the way I do — patiently, by phone, with the numbers in plain English. A final expense plan isn't really about a policy. It's about the week your family will go through, and whether money is one of the problems in it. My dad died of leukemia, and my brother died of cancer not long after — also with no coverage in place. Three of the closest losses of my life, and every one of them came with a bill and no plan.

Straight answers, before you ever call

Final expense insurance is a smaller whole life policy — most of my families choose $30,000–$45,000 — meant to cover funeral or cremation costs and the bills that come due right after a death. It's permanent: it never expires, and the premium never increases.

Most final expense plans don't require a medical exam. Approval is based on a set of health questions, and there are options for people with many common health conditions. When we talk, I'll tell you honestly what you can qualify for.

It depends on your wishes — burial or cremation, the kind of service you want, and any final bills you'd like covered rather than left behind. We'll add it up together on the call, and you'll see every number before you decide anything.

Most final expense policies use simplified underwriting — a health questionnaire instead of a medical exam — and there are options designed for people with common health conditions. What you qualify for depends on your age and health history, which is why I review each person’s situation individually. A free review costs nothing and tells you exactly where you stand.

Yes, cremation generally costs less than a traditional burial, mainly because there is no cemetery plot or burial vault required. But “less” is not “nothing” — a funeral with cremation still runs into the thousands. The right choice is the one that matches your wishes and your family’s budget, and it is far easier to decide while it is still just information.

The bill does not wait. Funeral homes need payment arrangements before services, Social Security pays a one-time death benefit of only $255, and employer life insurance often takes weeks to process — if it exists at all. So families end up using savings, credit cards, or a fundraiser page during the worst week of their lives. A small policy set up ahead of time is what keeps that week about family instead of money.
